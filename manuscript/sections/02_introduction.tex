\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Conservation decisions for a distributed archaeological landscape begin with a practical question: where should limited inspection capacity be directed, and what should field teams look for when they arrive? The answer becomes difficult when environmental and land-use pressures vary over space, respond to weather over different periods, and leave signals that are only partly observable from the ground or from satellites. Periodic site visits provide indispensable evidence about fabric and condition, but they cannot continuously cover every monument, drainage pathway, slope and surrounding land-cover transition. Inspection intervals may also be longest where access, staffing and analytical capacity are most constrained, so an absence of recent observations is not evidence that a component is stable. Earth observation can extend surveillance across a landscape, yet its measurements concern reflected energy and surface context rather than the physical condition of archaeological materials. It is most useful when it narrows a field programme from an undifferentiated inventory to a set of explicit, testable inspection priorities. A defensible prioritisation system must therefore combine evidence without erasing the distinction between remotely sensed pressure, environmental susceptibility and confirmed deterioration \citep{agapiou2020eo,xiao2018geoinformatics,romao2022risk,luo2023space}.

Three evidence systems are especially relevant, but they operate at different supports. Multi-temporal optical imagery records vegetation, wetness and built or bare-surface expression at the scale of mixed pixels. Digital elevation models describe landform, slope and potential flow convergence, which may influence where runoff, sediment or ponding should be examined. Gridded climate products characterise regional forcing and antecedent weather, but their cells are much coarser than the archaeological components and cannot legitimately be portrayed as local 30~m meteorology. Their temporal meanings also differ. A terrain value is comparatively static, a three-year image composite summarises a seasonal interval, and a weather window aggregates days or months before image acquisition. The analytical value lies in their complementarity, not in forcing them into a false common resolution. Satellite observations indicate where the surface context changed, terrain analysis indicates where movement of water or sediment may be favoured, and weather records indicate the temporal conditions under which an image was acquired. None of these sources, separately or together, establishes damage without an appropriate field response variable \citep{moore1991terrain,quinn1991flow,sesana2021climate,guzman2020monitoring}.

The need for this separation is pronounced at the Taxila World Heritage property in Punjab, Pakistan. Taxila was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 as a serial property whose archaeological remains include settlements, Buddhist monastic complexes and other components distributed across a heterogeneous landscape \citep{unesco_taxila}. The component record encompasses places formed in different periods and settings rather than replicas of one site type. This spatial and archaeological diversity creates a monitoring problem that differs from the inspection of one enclosed monument. Conditions around one component cannot be assumed to represent another, while development, vegetation, exposed ground, topography and drainage context may vary over short distances. A landscape signal may also originate outside a visible ruin while remaining relevant to access, drainage or encroachment inspection. The present inventory contains 18 component records. Seventeen have usable point geometry for spatial analysis, whereas Saraikala, prehistoric mound (139-002), remains in the documentary inventory but lacks resolved geometry. A landscape-scale comparison can support inspection planning across the mapped components, provided that point-centred analytical neighbourhoods are not mistaken for official property polygons, statutory protection zones or UNESCO buffers. That proviso is not terminological caution alone: it determines what area the numerical summaries actually represent and prevents an analytical convenience from acquiring a legal meaning.

Taxila is not an unstudied case. Khan et al. combined satellite observations with ground-based horizontal-to-vertical spectral-ratio measurements to document natural and anthropogenic threats and to strengthen hazard reporting for the property \citep{khan2022taxila}. That work is the closest methodological benchmark because it links spaceborne and field evidence at Taxila rather than extrapolating from a distant heritage setting. It demonstrates the value of placing remote observations within a site-specific assessment, but it does not answer every temporal or decision-analytic question. The present study addresses a different problem. It asks how landscape-pressure indicators vary over multiple comparable satellite epochs, how acquisition-window weather changes their interpretation, and how terrain, climate and spectral evidence can be converted into a transparent inspection sequence whose sensitivity is measured. It also asks whether the apparent leader remains distinct when spatial samples and weights change. The study does not repeat the earlier field measurements and cannot claim a stronger diagnosis of monument condition merely because it uses a larger computational workflow. The two designs are complementary: one contributes ground-linked hazard documentation, while the other examines temporal comparability and the uncertainty of prioritisation.

The broader literature establishes that satellite imagery and geographic information systems can reveal urban expansion, land-cover change, flood context and other pressures around archaeological sites \citep{hadjimitsis2013cyprus,agapiou2015paphos,agapiou2016paphos,liu2019angkor,lasaponara2011satellite}. It also exposes four persistent interpretive problems. First, a single date or endpoint difference may conceal seasonal state, temporary disturbance and recovery within the interval. A long archive is informative only when observations are placed on comparable support and their temporal sampling is stated. Second, indices such as the normalised difference vegetation index, modified normalised difference water index and normalised difference built-up index are responsive to surface properties but are not unique labels of vegetation stress, moisture processes, construction or archaeological damage. Their physical interpretations overlap in mixed, sparsely vegetated landscapes. Third, a composite score can count correlated indicators more than once and can appear exact even when its ranking depends on the chosen radius, factor set or weights. Fourth, pixel-wise random validation can overstate geographic transfer when nearby training and test observations share spatial structure \citep{woodcock1987scale,roberts2017cv,ploton2020spatial,stehman2019accuracy}. These are not independent technical details. Together they determine whether a map functions as a transparent screening aid or as an opaque assertion of condition.

These issues are consequential for conservation practice because a ranked map is a decision instrument, not merely a visual summary. A fixed ordering can direct scarce staff and resources, so uncertainty about that ordering should be visible before it is operationalised. A small numerical difference between the first and second component may be less important than their shared separation from the rest of the inventory. Scale sensitivity asks whether a component remains important when the analytical neighbourhood changes. Redundancy analysis asks whether multiple indicators provide independent information. Ablation asks which factor or domain controls the result. Weight perturbation asks whether reasonable alternative priorities reverse the ordering. Spatial resampling asks whether the apparent separation between components survives changes in the sampled landscape. Taken together, these analyses can replace a brittle league table with inspection tiers and mechanism-specific questions. They are well established in spatial multi-criteria analysis and predictive mapping, but they are seldom reported together in heritage screening studies \citep{malczewski2006gis,chen2010sensitivity,feizizadeh2014uncertainty,guerriero2022derwent,yang2023yongtai}.

This study therefore develops and applies the Climate-linked Heritage Inspection Prioritisation (CHIP) framework. CHIP is a transparent analytical framework that integrates multi-temporal satellite-derived relative landscape pressure, terrain and hydrological susceptibility, regional climate forcing and explicit uncertainty analysis to prioritise field inspection around mapped archaeological components. It is organised as an evidence chain rather than a single classification: source quality and common support precede indicator transformation; component aggregation precedes scoring; and the declared score is followed by sensitivity and uncertainty experiments. The word ``priority'' is used comparatively: it identifies where inspection should begin within the analysed inventory, not whether a component has crossed an absolute condition or legal-risk threshold. The 250, 500 and 1,000~m circles are analytical sampling neighbourhoods only. The framework does not infer annual damage probability, material degradation, structural deterioration or causal weather effects.

Five research questions organise the investigation. \textbf{RQ1} asks how satellite-derived relative landscape-pressure indicators varied across five retained Landsat epochs around the mapped Taxila components. \textbf{RQ2} asks what climate variability, long-term trends and acquisition-window conditions characterised the study period, and what descriptive, non-causal associations existed with spectral-pressure indicators. \textbf{RQ3} asks how satellite-derived pressure, terrain and hydrological susceptibility, and climate forcing combine to define relative field-inspection priority. \textbf{RQ4} asks how sensitive component rankings are to analytical scale, correlated indicators, factor and domain removal, weight perturbation and spatial block-bootstrap resampling. \textbf{RQ5} asks how well geographically separated models reproduce WorldCover-derived proxy land-cover labels, without implying heritage-condition accuracy. The questions are sequential. RQ1 and RQ2 establish the observational evidence and its temporal context; RQ3 converts that evidence into a declared decision model; RQ4 tests the stability of that model; and RQ5 examines a separate proxy-classification task under stricter geographic separation. This order prevents the model experiment from being used as retrospective validation of the priority ranking.

The resulting contribution is methodological and practical. It provides a traceable path from source observations to component priorities; a common-support, multi-epoch comparison that does not convert spectral change into damage; a ranking analysis that separates the declared fixed score from stability under scale, ablation, weighting and spatial uncertainty; and a buffered proxy-label experiment designed to limit geographic leakage. It also retains corrected, negative and inconclusive results in the evidence trail, including weather-product disagreement and overlap between leading component ranks. This treatment matters because inconclusive evidence can change a management recommendation even when it does not support a positive scientific claim. The intended output is therefore not only a map, but also an account of why a component was elevated, how confidently it was separated from alternatives and which observation could confirm or overturn that interpretation. Full experiment-status reporting and code-linked evidence make the analysis revisable when authoritative geometry, local meteorology or field observations become available. The conservation value lies in that capacity to learn from inspection, not in freezing the current score as a permanent classification. This design supports field-survey sequencing and the formulation of component-specific inspection questions. It does not replace professional condition assessment, engineering diagnosis, official boundary information or legal conservation decisions.

\section{Introduction}
Conservation of an archaeological landscape is, operationally, a scheduling
problem. A site authority with a few inspectors, a seasonal weather window and a
property spread over tens of square kilometres must decide which components to
visit this year and which to defer. That decision is rarely limited by a lack of
imagery, but by the absence of a defensible procedure for turning environmental
evidence into a visit order the authority can justify to a regulator, a funder or
a periodic-reporting cycle.

Systematic reviews of climate and cultural heritage identify this translation
step, not hazard detection, as the weak link between research and conservation
practice \citep{fatoric2017threatened,orr2021climate}. Deterioration mechanisms
are diverse and site-specific, from Arctic permafrost loss
\citep{hollesen2018arctic} to Mediterranean coastal erosion
\citep{reimann2018mediterranean} and inland fluvial hazard
\citep{liu2019angkor,arrighi2023flood}, and vulnerability depends on materials,
maintenance, drainage and microenvironment as well as forcing
\citep{sesana2021climate,sesana2020vulnerability,crowley2022risk}.

Earth observation and GIS supply the inputs such assessments require, with mature
workflows for landscape survey, hazard screening, disaster-risk management and
monitoring of designated properties in changing environments
\citep{lasaponara2011satellite,opitz2018trends,tapete2018geosciences,agapiou2020eo,luo2023space,guzman2020monitoring,agapiou2021dynamic,hadjimitsis2013cyprus},
increasingly with machine-learning support
\citep{argyrou2022review,yao2023overview,xiao2018geoinformatics}. Applications
reaching a component-level ordering typically combine indicators by weighted
aggregation and report the resulting index or zonation
\citep{yang2023yongtai,guerriero2022derwent,romao2022risk}.

What such outputs generally do not report is how much of the ordering is a
property of the landscape and how much is a property of the analysis. GIS
multicriteria methods make aggregation explicit, sensitivity analysis can expose
dependence on weights and spatial representation
\citep{malczewski2006gis,feizizadeh2014uncertainty,helton2006sensitivity}, and
rank-acceptability analysis describes which alternatives stay preferred under
uncertain criteria \citep{lahdelma2001smaa}. These tools are uncommon in heritage
prioritisation, yet the conditions motivating them are routine: correlated
spectral proxies duplicate information, overlapping neighbourhoods share
observations, inventory coordinates are imprecise, and domain weights are
declared rather than measured. A single ranking therefore leaves a site manager
unable to separate an exposed component from an artefact of a weighting choice.

Taxila makes these questions concrete: a serial inscription of 18 Gandharan
components in northern Punjab, Pakistan, in a rapidly urbanising corridor. Khan
et al. document damage at three components from ground geophysics and satellite
evidence \citep{khan2022taxila}; Butt et al. map land-cover transition and urban
encroachment across the property over 1990--2024 \citep{butt2025taxila}; and
Megarry et al. extend the relevance of surrounding land cover to
climate-vulnerability assessment for cultural World Heritage across the Indian
Subcontinent \citep{megarry2026landcover}. These establish that the conservation
problem is real and that landscape context carries information about it. They do
not establish which components a limited inspection programme should visit first,
nor how firmly that answer is held.

We develop CHIP to address that gap, with four contributions. First, a
hierarchical landscape--terrain rule exposes indicator influence through a
complete set of alternative aggregation rules and single-factor ablations, with
interval estimates on every rank comparison. Second, multiscale, shared-block,
positional and nested spatial--decision experiments separate persistent
priorities from representation-dependent ones. Third, and centrally, the joint
simulation admits an exact affine reconstruction that converts it into a balanced
full-factorial design, yielding rank-acceptability indices, central weights,
confidence factors, a decomposition of rank variance into decision and
spatial-support contributions, and an inspection-budget curve. Fourth, we
confront the resulting ordering with the independent published evidence of damage
and encroachment at Taxila, and report that confrontation whatever its outcome.

\section{Research aim}
The study identifies which Taxila components merit early field inspection, how
firmly that recommendation is held, and which assumption a manager would have to
resolve to sharpen it. It tests adverse spectral convergence against an analytic
independence reference, rank stability across neighbourhoods, aggregation rules
and uncertainty designs, the relative contribution of decision and spatial
assumptions to rank uncertainty, the inspection budget required for a stated
coverage probability, and the agreement between the resulting order and
independently published component-level evidence. Mathematical definitions
connect these experiments to reproducible component-level decisions.

\section{Introduction}
Archaeological landscapes are monitored through observations that differ in spatial scale, temporal coverage and relevance to conservation. A satellite pixel describes a land surface; a weather grid describes atmospheric conditions over a much larger area; a terrain model approximates pathways that may concentrate runoff. None directly observes the condition of a wall, stupa or buried deposit. Yet conservation teams must use incomplete evidence to decide where to inspect first. The practical challenge is to translate landscape observations into a defensible inspection sequence while retaining the limits of each observation.

Remote sensing and geographical information systems have documented development pressure around heritage clusters and supported multi-hazard assessments \citep{agapiou2015paphos,agapiou2016paphos,guerriero2022derwent}. At Taxila, Khan et al. combined ground-based and satellite evidence to assess risks to World Heritage components \citep{khan2022taxila}. That work establishes the local conservation problem and the importance of field observations. A further question concerns the stability of an inspection decision: would the same components remain priorities if the analyst changed the neighbourhood radius, removed a correlated indicator, or admitted uncertainty in spatial sampling? An assessment that supplies only a final map cannot answer this question.

Climate adds a second interpretive difficulty. Heritage exposure depends on material, maintenance, drainage and microenvironment, as well as meteorological forcing \citep{sesana2021climate,sesana2020vulnerability,crowley2022risk}. Annual weather summaries may describe conditions different from those prevailing before the satellite scenes used to construct an epoch. Gridded products may also disagree. Climate context therefore requires explicit alignment to acquisition dates and comparison across products before it can inform a conservation explanation. Five multi-year satellite composites do not provide sufficient temporal replication to identify a causal weather effect on archaeological condition.

Model evaluation presents a related problem. Nearby pixels can share both predictors and labels, making random partitions an optimistic representation of geographic transfer \citep{hijmans2012cv,meyer2018target,meyer2021aoa}. Spatial separation addresses part of this dependence, but it cannot turn a global land-cover product into independent field truth. A classifier that reproduces WorldCover labels may recognise vegetation or built surfaces without establishing which heritage component is deteriorating. This distinction remains necessary as model families become more capable.

We address these issues through an evidence-to-decision workflow, CHIP, that retains separate landscape, terrain, climate and proxy-evaluation paths. The methodological contribution is to evaluate the decision rule itself: indicator ablation identifies which observations control the ranking; spatial and positional experiments test dependence on sampling support; and crossed spatial--decision simulations quantify their combined effect. A separate seven-family land-cover comparison evaluates the spectral representation under geographic separation. These experiments connect component-level priorities to the evidence and assumptions that produce them, allowing inspection teams to distinguish broadly persistent priorities from priorities associated with a particular environmental mechanism.

\section{Research aim}
The study aims to identify which Taxila components merit early field inspection and which parts of that ordering depend on analytical choices. Specifically, it examines whether adverse spectral changes converge on common spatial support; whether weather interpretation changes with acquisition window or data product; whether component ranks remain stable across neighbourhood radii, indicator composition and resampling; and whether land-cover proxy agreement persists under buffered spatial evaluation. These questions link the computational evidence to a practical conservation task while keeping landscape screening distinct from direct assessment of heritage condition.

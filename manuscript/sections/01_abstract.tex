\begin{abstract}
Satellite monitoring can help allocate scarce field-inspection capacity across archaeological landscapes, but a precise ranking may conceal sensitivity to spatial support, correlated indicators and unverified environmental proxies. We developed the Climate-contextual Heritage Inspection Prioritisation (CHIP) framework to make those dependencies explicit at the Taxila World Heritage property, Pakistan. The analysis combined 55 Landsat scenes in five post-monsoon epochs, daily gridded weather for 1991--2025, approximately 30 m terrain data and 17 geolocated heritage components. Landscape pressure and terrain susceptibility were combined hierarchically, with climate retained at its native contextual support. Scale, weight, factor-ablation and spatial-resampling experiments tested the stability of inspection priorities. The common endpoint mask contained 386,280 cells; 16.93\% met at least two adverse spectral screening criteria and 1.42\% met all three. Giri had the highest fixed 500 m priority score, but its rank spanned 1--9 under the separate archived integrated-score weight experiment. The 500 and 1,000 m rankings agreed more closely than the 250 and 1,000 m rankings. Opposing precipitation trends in two gridded weather products limited climatic interpretation. A retrospective extension compared seven classifiers with buffered inner spatial validation; the development-selected \ExtensionModel{} achieved macro-F1 \ExtensionFOne{} on 4,188 outer-stripe proxy labels. This benchmark measures land-cover agreement, not heritage condition. CHIP provides a reproducible basis for selecting inspection tiers and targeted survey questions. Its conservation value rests on exposing unstable decisions and directing subsequent field validation, rather than assigning damage probabilities to monuments.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} cultural heritage; Taxila; remote sensing; spatial uncertainty; inspection prioritisation; reproducibility.

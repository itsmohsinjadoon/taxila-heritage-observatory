\begin{abstract}
Conservation authorities must allocate scarce inspection capacity across serial properties. Satellite, terrain and weather screening can order components by inferred pressure, but rarely tests whether that order survives analytical choices or agrees with independent condition evidence. We present the Climate-contextual Heritage Inspection Prioritisation (CHIP) framework and test it at the Taxila World Heritage property, Pakistan. Inputs comprise 55 Landsat scenes across five epochs, approximately 30~m terrain, daily weather for 1991--2025 and 17 of 18 inventory components. Among 386,280 common-support cells, 16.93\% met at least two adverse endpoint criteria versus 10.40\% expected under independence (ratio 1.63). Removing NDVI or NDBI retained rank concordance ($\rho>0.989$), whereas removing MNDWI gave $\rho=-0.280$ (95\% CI $-0.676$ to 0.242). Across eight aggregation rules, an oriented principal-component rule reversed the order ($\rho=-0.771$, $-0.925$ to $-0.403$). Exact affine reconstruction converted 5,000 simulation draws into a balanced 280,998-scenario design. No component's first-rank confidence factor exceeded 0.42; decision assumptions explained 45.8\% of rank variance versus 31.8\% for spatial support. Three inspection visits covered a scenario's highest three priorities in 17.9\% of cases, rising to 51.8\% with four. None of five components with published damage or encroachment fell within the structural CHIP core tier; the component with documented core-zone loss lacked a usable coordinate. CHIP screens moisture- and terrain-driven landscape pressure and should be combined with condition and encroachment records rather than used as their substitute.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Taxila; inspection prioritisation;
decision analysis under uncertainty; rank acceptability; remote sensing; reproducibility.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What CHIP demonstrates, and where its inference ends}\label{sec:discussion-answer}

The central result is not a diagnosis of monument condition. It is a transparent way to decide where limited field-inspection effort should be directed when several imperfect forms of spatial evidence must be considered together. Across Taxila, the satellite record identified selective rather than property-wide convergence of adverse surface indicators, terrain and drainage context changed the relative importance of components, and the identity of the highest-ranked component depended on analytical support and uncertainty treatment. The Climate-linked Heritage Inspection Prioritisation framework therefore answered the practical research problem by producing an inspectable chain from observations to a relative priority, while retaining the disagreements within that chain. This is narrower than the risk-assessment language often used in heritage geospatial studies \citep{hadjimitsis2013cyprus,agapiou2016paphos,yang2023yongtai}. A complete risk assessment would require evidence about hazards, exposed assets, vulnerability, condition and consequences \citep{romao2022risk}. CHIP supplies landscape-pressure and susceptibility evidence that can inform such an assessment, but does not replace it.

This distinction is especially important at Taxila. Khan et al. combined ground-based measurements and satellite observations to document natural and anthropogenic threats at the World Heritage property \citep{khan2022taxila}. The present analysis extends the landscape-monitoring dimension through five Landsat epochs, scene-matched climatic context, multiple analytical radii, indicator-redundancy controls, factor ablation and rank-distribution experiments. It does not reproduce the ground-based condition evidence of the earlier study, and it should not be read as a stronger condition diagnosis merely because it uses more computational experiments. Its contribution is different: it exposes how a field-inspection sequence changes when scale, weights, spatial sampling and coarse climatic support are made explicit. In operational terms, the framework is best used to form inspection tiers and questions for field teams, not to assign a final risk class remotely.

The results also show why several evidence domains were needed. A landscape-only ranking agreed weakly with the integrated ordering, whereas terrain-only ordering was substantially closer. Climate-only scores could not differentiate individual components because 17 mapped sites resolved to four weather cells. No single domain was therefore sufficient for all parts of the decision. Earth observation is well suited to repeatable landscape surveillance, and geoinformatics can organise dispersed heritage information for conservation planning \citep{agapiou2020eo,xiao2018geoinformatics,yao2023overview}. Their value here lies in screening and comparison. Direct inspection remains necessary to establish whether the mapped context corresponds to blocked drainage, erosion, vegetation encroachment, construction pressure, altered surface moisture, or no conservation-relevant change at all.

\subsection{Meaning of the multi-temporal landscape-pressure pattern}\label{sec:discussion-spectral}

The endpoint evidence indicates a landscape composed mainly of non-convergent cells with smaller, spatially concentrated zones in which two or three spectral criteria agreed. Common endpoint support covered 95.7371\% of the fixed grid, but only 16.9284\% of supported cells met at least two adverse-change criteria and 1.4220\% met all three. The scientific meaning of this contrast is that potential pressure signatures are localised and multidimensional. A reduced vegetation signal, reduced wetness expression and increased built or bare-surface response may coincide where surface cover has changed, but each index also responds to seasonal state, soil background, sensor characteristics and mixed pixels. Remote-sensing studies around Paphos similarly demonstrate the value of observing development and landscape context around heritage sites \citep{agapiou2015paphos,agapiou2016paphos}. CHIP adds a common-support endpoint screen and retains the resulting pattern as relative landscape evidence, rather than translating the convergent area into damaged heritage fabric.

The five-epoch design further cautions against treating the E2004 to E2024 difference as a continuous trajectory. Each epoch is a median composite of a three-year post-monsoon window, so it suppresses short-lived contamination and increases valid coverage, but it cannot reveal every intervening disturbance or recovery. Cross-sensor continuity research supports careful use of Landsat 7 and Landsat 8 reflectance and vegetation indices, while also showing why sensor transition should not be ignored \citep{roy2016landsat}. The heterogeneous trajectories observed across Taxila are therefore more credible as comparative landscape histories than as exact dates of change. A field team can use the maps to identify persistent or convergent locations, but attribution requires finer temporal imagery, documentary evidence and site observation.

The strong NDVI-pressure and NDBI-pressure rank correlation clarifies the structure of the spectral evidence. At 500 m, $\rho=0.971707$ meant that these transformations carried nearly the same component-ordering information, even though their physical interpretations are not identical. NDBI was designed to enhance built-up response but can include spectrally similar bare surfaces \citep{zha2003ndbi}; NDVI responds to vegetation abundance and condition but is not a direct land-use label. Treating them as independent equal-weight indicators would have counted a shared surface-cover gradient twice. Grouping them within one subdomain limited that amplification. The ablation results support this decision because removing either term barely changed the ranking, while removing MNDWI produced a severe reversal. The practical implication is not that one correlated index should automatically be discarded. It is that indicator families should be diagnosed empirically and weighted according to the independent decision information they retain.

MNDWI's influence requires equally cautious interpretation. The index was developed to enhance open-water features and suppress built-up noise \citep{xu2006mndwi}, but in an archaeological landscape its transformed neighbourhood summary can also reflect soil moisture, drainage-related surface conditions and mixtures of land cover. Its removal changed the ordering more than removal of NDVI or NDBI, which shows that it supplied a distinct axis in this implementation. It does not establish water-related deterioration at the components that moved most. Those movements identify locations where inspection should explicitly check drainage condition, ponding, erosion pathways, dampness indicators and the local land-cover setting. In this sense, sensitivity is decision information: the factor that controls a component's priority also specifies what field evidence is needed to confirm or reject the remote interpretation.

\subsection{Climate forcing and temporal ambiguity}\label{sec:discussion-climate}

Within Open-Meteo, the 1991 to 2025 series described increasing mean temperature and declining annual precipitation, heavy-precipitation-day frequency and soil moisture. The block-bootstrap intervals and false-discovery-rate adjustment make these statements more defensible than uncorrected linear fits, while the non-significant annual and monsoon precipitation-extreme metrics prevent a simple narrative of uniformly intensifying rainfall. Reviews of climate effects on cultural heritage describe multiple pathways involving heat, moisture, extreme precipitation and interacting environmental processes \citep{sesana2021climate}. The Taxila results provide regional forcing context for investigating those pathways, but no field response variable was available. Consequently, the trends cannot be converted into deterioration rates, hazard probabilities or causal effects on archaeological material.

The acquisition-window analysis resolves an important temporal ambiguity. E2024 was not the wettest epoch over the 30, 90 or 365 days preceding retained Landsat scenes. A calendar-year description had obscured the weather actually preceding image acquisition. This matters because vegetation, surface wetness and bare-ground expression can respond over different antecedent periods. It also means that a single annual climate descriptor should not be used to explain a multi-scene spectral composite. The corrected interpretation strengthens the analytical workflow because it rejects a convenient but unsupported story. For conservation monitoring, image selection and field scheduling should therefore retain acquisition dates and antecedent weather windows, particularly where wetness-sensitive indicators influence priority.

The disagreement between Open-Meteo and NASA POWER is a larger limitation than the uncertainty interval around either product alone. Temperature series were strongly rank-correlated at daily resolution, although they differed in level, while annual precipitation agreement was weak and the inferred long-term directions opposed one another. These products have different modelling, forcing, spatial support and bias characteristics. Neither was validated against a local station in this study. It would therefore be inappropriate to select the product that best supports a preferred conservation narrative. The defensible conclusion is product sensitivity. Climate-informed inspection can use the shared temporal context provisionally, but claims about local hydroclimatic change require comparison with quality-controlled station observations or another locally evaluated data source.

The weather-spectral correlations reinforce this restraint. With only five independent Landsat epochs, even coefficients as large as $|\rho|=0.80$ can change markedly when one epoch is omitted. The leave-one-epoch ranges show that the apparent direction is not stable enough for attribution, and formal significance testing would add little information at this sample size. The coefficients should be treated as hypotheses about lag structure, not as estimated climatic effects. A stronger design would increase the number of temporally independent image composites, predefine lag windows and test whether associations remain after accounting for season, sensor and spatial dependence. Until then, CHIP uses climate as forcing context and a prompt for seasonally informed inspection, not as a fine-resolution causal predictor.

\subsection{Terrain, hydrology and differentiated component priority}\label{sec:discussion-terrain}

Terrain and hydrological variables changed component priority because they represent processes that spectral surface indicators cannot fully observe. Slope affects the potential for runoff acceleration and sediment movement; contributing area and wetness proxies identify convergent topographic settings; drainage proximity indicates possible connection to flow pathways. These variables are widely used to structure hydrological interpretation from digital elevation models \citep{beven1979topmodel,tarboton1997flow,farr2007srtm}. In CHIP they are susceptibility descriptors, not event inventories or calibrated erosion probabilities. Their spatial non-coincidence with spectral convergence is scientifically useful because it separates a visible surface signal from the landform context through which water and sediment may move.

The difference between Sirkap, Bhallar and Giri illustrates the resulting decision logic. Landscape-only evidence placed Sirkap first, terrain-only evidence placed Bhallar first, and the declared 500 m integrated score placed the Giri complex first. These are not competing claims about which component is most damaged. They are answers to different screening questions. Sirkap merits attention for its relative surface-cover context, Bhallar for the combination of terrain and hydrological factors, and Giri for the equal-domain integrated score. Studies at Angkor and the Derwent Valley Mills show how hydrological or multi-hazard susceptibility mapping can support heritage management \citep{liu2019angkor,guerriero2022derwent}. The present framework is more conservative because it lacks a validated local event or condition response, so it converts susceptibility into inspection prompts rather than a hazard or risk class.

The coarse climate support explains why climate did not alter the local ordering. Assigning the same weather-cell value to several components is honest about the source resolution, but it cannot represent small-scale rainfall, drainage or microclimatic differences. Artificially resampling those values to 30 m would have created cartographic detail without new information. The zero rank change after removing climate is therefore not evidence that climate is irrelevant. It shows that the selected product supports regional temporal context rather than component-scale discrimination. Local differentiation must come from terrain, landscape observations and future field or sensor measurements, while climate can inform the timing and type of inspection across the property.

\subsection{Decision consequences of scale and rank uncertainty}\label{sec:discussion-uncertainty}

Analytical scale materially changed the question being asked. A 250 m circle emphasises the immediate setting, 500 m balances local and surrounding context, and 1,000 m includes a broader landscape in which infrastructure, drainage and land-cover patterns may differ. The high agreement between 500 and 1,000 m ($\rho=0.91846$) indicates a shared broad ordering, but the lower 250 to 1,000 m agreement ($\rho=0.71078$), ten movements of at least three ranks and a maximum movement of eight positions show that apparent priority is not scale-free. The circles are analytical supports, not official UNESCO boundaries or legal buffers. Management use should either select a radius that matches a stated inspection question or examine all three scales before allocating resources.

Fixed ranking and ranking stability must likewise be separated. The 500 m point estimate placed Giri first, but 50,000 weight draws gave it a 95\% rank interval from 1 to 9. Spatial block resampling shifted the median leader to Bhallar, while Giri retained a high top-three probability and an overlapping rank distribution. These results do not invalidate the fixed score. They show that the fixed score answers one conditional question: what ordering results from the declared data, radius and equal-domain architecture? Weight perturbation asks whether reasonable preference variation changes that ordering, and the spatial bootstrap asks whether the sampled spatial pattern supports it consistently. A conservation decision should use all three answers.

The strongest operational response is to replace a winner-takes-all league table with priority tiers. Giri, Bhallar, Giri Mosque and tombs, and other components with substantial top-tier support should form an initial inspection group. Within that group, the domain profile can determine inspection emphasis. A component elevated by terrain should receive drainage and erosion-pathway checks, while one elevated by landscape pressure should receive land-cover, encroachment and surface-context checks. Lower-ranked components should not be treated as safe, since ranks are relative to this inventory and do not measure an absolute condition threshold. Tiering also makes the programme less sensitive to small numerical score differences and easier to update after field evidence is collected.

Ablation reveals where that update effort is most valuable. The ranking depended strongly on MNDWI, slope and drainage proximity, while NDVI and NDBI were largely redundant in rank terms. This pattern identifies both leverage and fragility. If field observations show that a wetness transformation is poorly related to relevant conditions at Taxila, a major part of the ordering would need revision. Conversely, confirming drainage and wetness mechanisms would increase confidence in the present prioritisation. Multi-criteria heritage studies commonly integrate several indicators and expert-derived weights \citep{guerriero2022derwent,yang2023yongtai}; the present analysis contributes an explicit test of what happens when factors, domains and weights change. Such tests should accompany, rather than follow, the publication of any operational priority map.

\subsection{Interpreting the proxy-label experiment}\label{sec:discussion-proxy}

The buffered modelling experiment demonstrated that Landsat predictors could reproduce WorldCover-derived proxy land-cover labels with useful but imperfect geographic transfer. The development-selected multilayer perceptron achieved macro-F1 0.830795 on the contiguous outer test region, with a block-bootstrap interval of 0.700054 to 0.859730. Logistic regression scored higher on that same outer set, but promoting it after observing test performance would compromise the test's role. This distinction between prospective model selection and retrospective best-score reporting is essential. The lower primary result relative to the less restrictive legacy split is also scientifically informative because spatial separation removed nearby samples that could otherwise make transfer appear easier.

Good practice in land-cover accuracy assessment requires reference data and sampling designs that support the intended accuracy claim \citep{olofsson2014accuracy}. Here the response labels came from WorldCover, not independent field interpretation, and the experiment therefore measures proxy agreement only. High AUROC or low calibration error cannot change that evidential status. The model may learn stable land-cover patterns, class boundaries inherited from WorldCover, or both. It does not validate archaeological condition, the CHIP score or any damage class. Its practical role is limited to testing whether the spectral feature space contains geographically transferable information relevant to a land-cover proxy and to identifying where automated screening may require review.

The model results nevertheless affect the design of future validation. Performance intervals widened under block resampling, confusion remained class dependent, and model ordering varied by metric. A field campaign should therefore avoid validating only easy, high-confidence pixels. It should sample across components, proxy classes, predicted uncertainty, spatial blocks and priority tiers. Independent condition observations should be recorded separately from land cover so that a future model can answer a genuinely heritage-specific question. Until such labels exist, proxy classification can support map interpretation and data-quality screening, but it should remain outside the evidential basis for claims about monument condition.

\subsection{Field-inspection and conservation-management implications}\label{sec:discussion-implications}

CHIP can support a staged inspection programme. First, managers can select a scale appropriate to the decision and identify components that remain in the upper tier across fixed and uncertainty summaries. Second, the factor profile can define a short site-specific checklist covering drainage obstruction, erosion or sediment pathways, vegetation change, exposed soil, construction context and surface wetness. Third, scene-specific weather histories can guide the timing of repeat visits, especially after locally verified rainfall events or during comparable seasonal conditions. Finally, field observations can be returned to the evidence register so that confirmed conditions, rejected interpretations and unresolved cases become explicit inputs to the next ranking cycle.

This use is consistent with the broader role proposed for Earth observation and GIS in cultural-heritage disaster management: repeated spatial screening can help target scarce expertise, but it must be connected to conservation knowledge and on-site assessment \citep{agapiou2020eo,yao2023overview}. For Taxila, the framework can also support coordination between heritage managers, environmental agencies and remote-sensing analysts. The map provides a common spatial agenda, while the uncertainty products prevent a false appearance of precision. Neither the analytical neighbourhoods nor their scores change legal designation, management authority or protection boundaries.

The results also suggest a monitoring sequence rather than a one-time survey. High-priority components should receive the earliest field inspection, but medium and lower tiers require scheduled surveillance because the ranking is relative and landscape conditions can change. Repeat satellite composites should be produced with consistent quality controls, supplemented by targeted higher-resolution imagery where a specific mechanism is suspected. After field verification, factor weights and transformations can be revised transparently. This adaptive cycle would turn CHIP from a static ranking into a documented learning process without implying that the current analysis has already validated the relevant deterioration mechanisms.

\subsection{Limitations and next scientific step}\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

Several limitations define the boundary of the present study. Only five independent Landsat epochs were available for temporal association, and each represented a three-year composite rather than a single condition survey. Landsat pixels mix archaeological fabric with soil, vegetation, paths and surrounding land cover. Cross-sensor continuity was addressed procedurally but cannot eliminate all residual differences. The endpoint convergence thresholds were empirical screening rules, not physically calibrated damage thresholds. NDVI-pressure and NDBI-pressure remained highly correlated even after hierarchical grouping, and the strong influence of MNDWI makes local interpretation of wetness-related signals a priority for verification.

Spatial support was also incomplete. The inventory retained Saraikala, but its unresolved geometry excluded it from coordinate-dependent summaries and rankings. The remaining components were represented by points and circular neighbourhoods because harmonised official property polygons were not available to the analysis. Those circles cross heterogeneous surroundings and must not be mistaken for component boundaries, legal buffers or protection zones. Terrain and drainage variables came from an approximately 30 m elevation product and simplified flow routing. They can reveal relative landform context but not drainage infrastructure, subsurface pathways, hydraulic capacity or observed flood and erosion events.

Climate evidence was limited to four Open-Meteo cells and a site-level NASA POWER comparison without local station validation. Product disagreement, especially for precipitation, prevents a single definitive regional trend interpretation. The weather variables were intentionally not downscaled to Landsat resolution, which preserved support honesty but limited component differentiation. The optional ancillary-hazard analysis was not executed because a harmonised, validated event inventory was not available. Accordingly, the study does not provide a complete multi-hazard or disaster-risk assessment, and it cannot forecast a future alarming condition for individual monuments.

The priority architecture contains normative choices. Equal-domain weighting is transparent but not uniquely correct; Dirichlet perturbation explored that choice but did not represent every possible stakeholder preference. Spatial block bootstrap captured one form of sampling sensitivity, not uncertainty in source measurement, transformations, thresholds or missing geometry. The proxy model used WorldCover-derived labels, not independent land-cover interpretation or condition records, and its contiguous holdout tested one geographic transfer direction. These constraints mean that neither the model nor the ranking can be interpreted as independent validation of heritage condition.

The next critical step is a stratified, component-level field campaign designed directly from the uncertainty results. Sampling should include the overlapping high-priority group, components that change markedly with radius or ablation, and lower-ranked comparators. At each location, teams should document fabric condition separately from surrounding land cover; inspect drainage, erosion, moisture and encroachment mechanisms; record georeferenced photographs and repeatable measurements; and link observations to acquisition-date weather. Local meteorological records and official component polygons should be incorporated where obtainable, and Saraikala's geometry should be resolved through authoritative documentation. These additions would allow future work to test whether CHIP indicators predict observed inspection findings, recalibrate priorities and determine which remote signals have conservation meaning. Until that validation is completed, the present framework remains a reproducible and uncertainty-aware guide for relative field-inspection sequencing.
